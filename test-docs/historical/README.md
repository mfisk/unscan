# Historical Typography Test Documents

Scanned documents spanning ~500 years of typographic history, assembled as
ground-truth test cases for unprint's font detection pipeline. Each file
represents a distinct typeface era, with known provenance and high-confidence
font identification.

## Inventory

### 1. `1545-garamond-estienne-bible.pdf`
- **Era:** French Renaissance, 1540s
- **Expected typeface:** Garamond (Claude Garamond's roman types, cut for Robert Estienne)
- **Document:** *Biblia: quid in hac editione praestitum sit…* — Robert Estienne's second octavo Bible, Paris, 1545
- **Source:** Internet Archive — `bibliaquidinhac00vatagoog` (Google-digitized)
- **URL:** https://archive.org/details/bibliaquidinhac00vatagoog
- **Pages:** 3 representative pages (n20, n50, n100) — body text in Garamond roman
- **Confidence:** ★★★★★ HIGH — Robert Estienne was Garamond's primary patron. His 1540s editions are the canonical showcase of original Garamond punches. Typography historians (Vervliet, Warde) confirm the types.
- **Notes:** The letterforms in this book are the direct ancestors of all modern Garamond revivals (EB Garamond, Adobe Garamond, etc.). EB Garamond is specifically modeled on the 1592 Egenolff-Berner specimen, which preserved Garamond's original matrices.

### 2. `1734-caslon-type-specimen.pdf`
- **Era:** English Old Style, 1730s–1790s
- **Expected typeface:** Caslon (William Caslon's foundry types)
- **Document:** *A Specimen of Printing Types* — Caslon type foundry specimen book
- **Source:** Internet Archive — `specimenofprinti00casl` (St Bride Library digitization)
- **URL:** https://archive.org/details/specimenofprinti00casl
- **Pages:** 3 pages (pp. 5–7) extracted from 220-page specimen
- **Confidence:** ★★★★★ HIGH — This IS the Caslon specimen. Direct type display from the foundry William Caslon established. Shows the full range of Caslon types in all sizes.
- **Notes:** William Caslon I (1692–1766) created the first distinctly English typeface, liberating English printing from Dutch type imports. His Caslon Old Style dominated English-language printing for a century.

### 3. `1757-baskerville-virgil.pdf`
- **Era:** Transitional, 1757
- **Expected typeface:** Baskerville (John Baskerville's transitional serif)
- **Document:** *Publii Virgilii Maronis Bucolica, Georgica et Aeneis* — John Baskerville's landmark Virgil, Birmingham, 1757
- **Source:** Internet Archive — `publiivirgiliima00virg_0`
- **URL:** https://archive.org/details/publiivirgiliima00virg_0
- **Pages:** 3 representative pages (n15, n100, n200) from 540-page scan
- **Confidence:** ★★★★★ HIGH — This is the first book from Baskerville's press and the debut of his revolutionary typeface. Also the first book ever printed on wove paper (which Baskerville invented). Confirmed by metadata: "Baskerville, John, 1706-1775, printer."
- **Notes:** Baskerville is the archetype of "transitional" type — bridging Caslon's Old Style and Bodoni's Modern. Higher contrast, wider letterforms, sharper serifs. Benjamin Franklin was an admirer and correspondent.

### 4. `1776-caslon-dunlap-broadside.pdf`
- **Era:** Colonial American, 1776
- **Expected typeface:** Caslon (the font of the American Revolution)
- **Document:** The Dunlap Broadside — first printing of the Declaration of Independence, by John Dunlap, Philadelphia, July 4, 1776
- **Source:** Library of Congress — IIIF image service
- **URL:** https://www.loc.gov/item/2004pe76546/
- **Pages:** 1 page (the broadside itself)
- **Confidence:** ★★★★☆ HIGH — The Dunlap Broadside was set in Caslon, the dominant English-language typeface of the era. Typography historians confirm this. The LoC image is a high-quality scan of one of the 26 surviving copies.
- **Notes:** The Declaration was deliberately set in Caslon — the standard "serious" typeface of colonial printing. King George III would have recognized it immediately.

### 5. `1818-bodoni-manuale-tipografico.pdf`
- **Era:** Modern/Didone, 1818
- **Expected typeface:** Bodoni (Giambattista Bodoni's Modern types)
- **Document:** *Manuale Tipografico* — Bodoni's posthumous type specimen masterwork, Parma, 1818
- **Source:** Internet Archive — `5873-1` (St Bride Library 125th anniversary digitization)
- **URL:** https://archive.org/details/5873-1
- **Pages:** 3 representative pages (n20, n80, n120) from the full specimen
- **Confidence:** ★★★★★ HIGH — The *Manuale Tipografico* IS Bodoni. Described as "probably the most elaborate specimen that the world has ever seen" (Updike). Contains 250+ type specimens in 8 languages. About 290 copies were printed.
- **Notes:** Bodoni (1740–1813) took Baskerville's high-contrast approach to its mathematical extreme: razor-thin hairlines, flat unbracketed serifs, extreme thick/thin contrast. His types define the "Modern" or "Didone" classification.

### 6. `1870s-victorian-slab-serif-specimen.pdf`
- **Era:** Victorian/Industrial, 1870s–1880s
- **Expected typeface:** Various slab serifs, fat faces, and display types (Clarendon-era wood type)
- **Document:** *Specimens of Types & Borders and Illustrated Catalogue* — H.W. Caslon & Co. (later Caslon foundry), London
- **Source:** Internet Archive — `specimensoftypes00hwcarich`
- **URL:** https://archive.org/details/specimensoftypes00hwcarich
- **Pages:** 3 pages (n10, n40, n80) showing display and advertising types
- **Confidence:** ★★★★☆ HIGH — H.W. Caslon & Co. was the continuation of the original Caslon foundry. This catalog shows the full range of Victorian-era display types: Egyptian/slab serifs, fat faces, ornamental types, and wood type specimens used in advertising broadsides and posters.
- **Notes:** The Industrial Revolution created a demand for big, bold display type. These are the ancestors of Rockwell, Clarendon, and other slab serifs. Many were cut in wood rather than metal for large sizes.

### 7. `1923-bauhaus-weimar-catalog.pdf`
- **Era:** Modernist/Geometric sans, 1920s
- **Expected typeface:** Geometric sans-serif types (Bauhaus typography — Universal-type, pre-Futura geometric forms)
- **Document:** *Staatliches Bauhaus in Weimar 1919–1923* — Official Bauhaus exhibition catalog
- **Source:** Internet Archive — `staatliches-bauhaus-in-weimar-1923-staatliches-bauhaus-in-weimar-1919-bis-1923`
- **URL:** https://archive.org/details/staatliches-bauhaus-in-weimar-1923-staatliches-bauhaus-in-weimar-1919-bis-1923
- **Pages:** 3 pages (n10, n30, n60)
- **Confidence:** ★★★☆☆ MEDIUM — The Bauhaus used various geometric and sans-serif types. Herbert Bayer's "Universal" alphabet and the school's preference for geometric sans-serifs anticipated Paul Renner's Futura (1927). The exact typefaces vary by page — some are custom Bauhaus designs.
- **Notes:** The Bauhaus rejected serifs as ornamental. Their typography favored clarity, geometry, and lowercase-only text. This catalog is a primary document of the movement that shaped all geometric sans-serif design.

### 8. `1961-courier-eisenhower-farewell.pdf`
- **Era:** Typewriter/Monospace, 1950s–1960s
- **Expected typeface:** Courier or Pica (typewriter monospace, likely IBM Executive or Selectric)
- **Document:** Eisenhower Farewell Address — White House press release, January 17, 1961
- **Source:** Eisenhower Presidential Library
- **URL:** https://www.eisenhowerlibrary.gov/
- **Pages:** 4 pages — typewritten government document
- **Confidence:** ★★★★☆ HIGH — White House press releases of this era were produced on IBM electric typewriters. The typeface is period government-standard monospace — either Courier (if post-Selectric) or Pica/Elite type.
- **Notes:** This is THE farewell address — "beware the military-industrial complex." Typewritten on White House stationery, a perfect specimen of the monospace era when every US government document was produced on typewriters.

### 10. `1970-tnr-irs-1040.pdf`
- **Era:** Times New Roman / Government forms, 1970
- **Expected typeface:** Times New Roman or similar serif (IRS standard body type)
- **Document:** IRS Form 1040 — U.S. Individual Income Tax Return, 1970
- **Source:** IRS prior-year forms archive
- **URL:** https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-prior/f1040--1970.pdf
- **Pages:** 2 pages
- **Confidence:** ★★★☆☆ MEDIUM — IRS forms have historically used variants of Times New Roman and Helvetica. The exact typeface used for 1970 forms may be a proprietary IRS variant, but the roman body text is TNR-adjacent.
- **Notes:** Government forms are excellent unprint test cases because they mix multiple font sizes, bold/regular, form fields, and fine print — all challenges for font detection.

### 11. `1976-helvetica-nasa-standards.pdf`
- **Era:** Helvetica/Swiss Style, 1976
- **Expected typeface:** Helvetica (and Futura for the NASA logotype)
- **Document:** *NASA Graphics Standards Manual* — The "worm era" visual identity bible, January 1976
- **Source:** Internet Archive — `NASA_Graphics_Standards_Manual`
- **URL:** https://archive.org/details/NASA_Graphics_Standards_Manual
- **Pages:** 3 pages (n10, n30, n60) — typography specifications and usage examples
- **Confidence:** ★★★★★ HIGH — The NASA GSM literally specifies Helvetica as the agency's standard typeface. Pages include Helvetica specimen charts, size specifications, and usage guidelines. The manual itself is set in Helvetica.
- **Notes:** This is the Helvetica holy grail. When NASA adopted the "worm" logo in 1975, they mandated Helvetica for all communications. The GSM shows Helvetica in every weight and size, with exact specifications — perfect for font matching validation.

## Not Yet Covered (Candidates for Future Additions)

- **1920s Futura** — Paul Renner's geometric sans (1927). Need a scanned Bauer catalog or early Bauhaus publication specifically using Futura.
- **1960s Helvetica in print** — Swiss periodicals like *Neue Grafik* (1958–1965) would be perfect but are not freely available digitized.
- **2000s ClearType** — Microsoft Calibri/Cambria era. Hard to find scanned specimens since these are digital-native fonts.
- **2010s Google Fonts** — Roboto, Open Sans, Source Serif Pro. These appear in born-digital PDFs rather than scanned documents.
